An interval is a ratio

Pitch is how fast something vibrates, measured in hertz (Hz): vibrations per second. An interval is the distance between two pitches. To build one, multiply the low note's frequency by a fraction. Multiply by 2 and you get an octave; multiply by 3/2 and you get a perfect fifth.

The harmonic series

A plucked string or a column of air doesn't vibrate at just one speed. It also vibrates in halves, thirds, quarters and so on, which adds quieter tones at 2×, 3×, 4× the base frequency. These are the harmonics (or overtones). Every interval with a simple ratio is hiding in here: harmonics 2 → 3 make a fifth (3 : 2), 4 → 5 make a major third (5 : 4).

Go deeper: why harmonics make timbre

"All together" plays harmonics 1 to 8 as pure sine waves at once. Your ear blends them into one note with a buzzy, brass-like color. The mix of harmonics, and how loud each one is, is what makes a flute, a violin and a clarinet sound different on the same note. That mix is called timbre.

Higher up the ladder the steps get smaller: 2 → 3 is a fifth, but 15 → 16 is only a half step. Some harmonics (7, 11, 13, 14) fall between the piano keys, because the piano's tuning is built from 12 equal steps, not from the harmonic series.

Beats: hear when it's in tune

When two tones are almost a pure ratio, the sound wobbles louder and softer. Those wobbles are called beats. The closer you get, the slower they go. At the exact ratio the beats stop and the sound locks into place. This is how musicians tune by ear.

Just vs equal: two ways to tune

Just intonation tunes every interval to a pure whole-number ratio, like 5 : 4. It sounds smooth, but only works well in one key. Equal temperament, the piano's tuning, splits the octave into 12 identical half steps so every key works the same. The price: every interval except the octave is slightly off pure.
