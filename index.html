        What is temperament?

        1. The problem: a piano can't tune every interval pure in every key.
        2. So we use equal temperament: the octave is split into 12 equal half steps.
        3. ✓ Pro: every key sounds the same, so we can play in all 12 keys.
        4. ✗ Con: the notes no longer come from ratios, so most intervals are a little off.
        5. For example:
          • The piano's fifth is 2 cents too low. Raise it 2 cents to get the pure 3/2.
          • The piano's major third is 14 cents too high. Lower it 14 cents to get the pure 5/4.
        6. That's why your band director says "lower the third": it makes the chord pure.

        Going further

        • Each equal half step multiplies the pitch by the same number:
        21/1212th root of 2=1.0595one half step
        • 12 of them multiply to exactly 2: one octave.
        • Why not pure? Stack 12 pure fifths: (3/2)12 = 129.7. Seven octaves: 27 = 128.
        • They miss by about 23 cents (the Pythagorean comma). Some intervals have to give a little.
        • Equal temperament spreads that little bit evenly over every fifth.

        What are beats?

        • Play 220 Hz and 222 Hz together.
        • The waves slide in and out of step.
        • In step = loud. Out of step = soft.
        • That happens 2 times every second: you hear 2 beats.
        • Beats per second = the difference: 222 − 220 = 2.
        • No beats = perfectly in tune.

        Going further

        • For a fifth, the beats come from harmonics: the low note × 3 should equal the high note × 2.
        • Beats per second = 2 × high − 3 × low. For any ratio top/bottom: bottom × high − top × low.
        • Pure sine waves have no harmonics, so a fifth of sine waves barely beats. Try the sound switch.
        • The piano's thirds are not pure, so they always beat a little. Try "Jump to the piano's version" on the major third.

        What is an interval?

        • An interval is the distance between two notes.
        • To make an interval, multiply by a fraction.
        • Hertz (Hz) means vibrations per second.
        • Orchestras tune to A = 440 Hz.
        • Cut it in half and you get A again, one octave lower:
        440 HzA4÷ 2 → 220 HzA3÷ 2 → 110 HzA2

        Going further

        • Stack two intervals by multiplying their ratios:
        54major 3rd× 65minor 3rd= 3020 = 32fifth
        • Cents measure interval size. 100 cents = one piano half step.
        • Pure ratios don't land exactly on piano keys. That's the small ± cents on some tones.
        • Two tones blend when their harmonics match. In a fifth, low × 3 = high × 2.

        Touch the string

        • Touch a string lightly at 1/3 of its length.
        • It splits into 3 equal parts and plays harmonic 3.
        • The fraction tells you where your finger goes.
        Touch lightly → a harmonic
        • Your finger doesn't stop the string.
        • The whole string keeps vibrating, but it must stay still under your finger.
        • So it vibrates in equal parts that fit: 3 parts at 1/3.
        • 3 parts, each 3× faster → harmonic 3.
        Press down → an interval
        • Your finger stops the string, like a fret.
        • Only the piece on the other side vibrates, as one piece.
        • A shorter piece vibrates faster → a higher note.
        • At 1/3, the 2/3 piece plays a fifth above the open string.

        Find the intervals in the harmonics

        • Put one harmonic on top of another. Their numbers make the ratio.
        • Harmonic 5 over harmonic 4 = 5/4, a major third.
        • Tap one to hear both harmonics together.

        What are harmonics?

        • A string doesn't only vibrate as a whole.
        • It also vibrates in halves, thirds, quarters…
        • Each part makes a higher tone: 2×, 3×, 4× the starting tone.
        • These are the harmonics. You hear them all at once, blended into one note.

        Going further

        • The steps get smaller as you climb: 2/1 is an octave, 16/15 is only a half step.
        • Harmonics 7, 11, 13 and 14 fall between piano keys. They're the orange ¢ numbers.
        • The mix of harmonics is why a flute and a trumpet sound different on the same note.
